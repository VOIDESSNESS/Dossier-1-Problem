Historical Record

A CENTURY OF BROKEN PROMISES

Nigeria's power sector has been failing for generations. Each era brought new announcements, new budgets, and new disappointments. Below is the documented history of a nation's light being extinguished — one policy failure at a time.

1896
First Electricity in Lagos — Colonial Infrastructure, Colonial Priorities
The colonial administration established Nigeria's first electricity supply in Lagos, serving government buildings and elite residences. Infrastructure was designed for resource extraction, not national development. The foundation of structural inequality was embedded from the very inception of the grid.
Colonial era — structural inequity begins
1951
Electricity Corporation of Nigeria (ECN) Established
Nigeria's first unified electricity authority expanded the grid but remained concentrated in urban centres. Rural Nigeria — the majority of the population — remained in complete darkness. The pattern of urban prioritisation that persists today was institutionalised here.
National institution formed — rural exclusion entrenched
1972
NEPA Formed — "Never Expect Power Always"
NEPA (National Electric Power Authority) was formed during the oil boom. Despite unprecedented petroleum revenues, power infrastructure investment remained negligible. The oil money was not redirected into sustainable energy. Nigerians coined the institution's sardonic alternative acronym — a cultural marker of deepening, permanent frustration that would outlast NEPA itself by decades.
Chronic underfunding despite oil wealth
1999
Return to Democracy — No Power Dividend
Nigeria transitioned to civilian rule with enormous expectations. The Obasanjo administration inherited a grid producing under 2,000 MW for 120 million people. Multiple "Emergency Power Programmes" followed with billions in federal spending. Generation barely moved. Each administration's power budget became a euphemism for misappropriated public funds. The democratic dividend never illuminated a single additional home.
Democracy without electricity
2005
Electric Power Sector Reform Act (EPSR Act)
The landmark EPSR Act created a framework for unbundling NEPA into generation companies (GenCos), the Transmission Company of Nigeria (TCN), and distribution companies (DisCos). It established NERC as the independent regulator and mapped a 15-year privatisation roadmap. On paper, it was a sound structural reform. In execution, it would be catastrophically undermined by implementation failures, political interference, and corruption at every stage.
Legal reform framework — implementation to follow
2013
Privatisation of DisCos and GenCos — Sale Without Standards
The Jonathan administration completed the privatisation of 11 DisCos and 6 GenCos for approximately $2.5 billion. New private owners inherited dilapidated infrastructure and were inadequately held to performance benchmarks. Metering gaps exceeded 60%. Technical and commercial losses remained above 35%. The privatisation transferred asset ownership without transferring accountability — a structurally flawed transaction whose consequences Nigerians still bear daily.
Private ownership without performance accountability
2016–2022
Over 100 National Grid Collapses
Between 2016 and 2022, Nigeria's national grid collapsed more than 100 times. Each collapse plunged the entire country into simultaneous darkness — hospitals lost power mid-procedure, businesses lost millions in production hours, cold chains failed, and perishable goods spoiled nationwide. The TCN cited aging infrastructure, gas supply shortfalls, and vandalism as causes. Each collapse lasted hours to days. The grid became measurably less reliable with each passing year, not more.
100+ collapses in 6 years
2023
Major Tariff Hikes — Payment Without Performance
Facing fiscal pressure and multilateral lender conditions tied to subsidy removal, the Tinubu administration approved significant electricity tariff increases — particularly for Band A consumers, now paying ₦225–280/kWh. Customers pay rates approaching international premium levels while receiving fewer than 10 hours of supply daily. The increase was not paired with any measurable improvement in generation or distribution. Nigerians were asked to pay exponentially more for demonstrably worse service.
Tariffs up 200%+; supply unchanged
2024–2025
Crisis Continues — The Transformation Window Opens
As of 2025, Nigeria's grid continues to experience total and partial collapses. Generation hovers at 3,500–5,500 MW — identical to a decade ago. The World Bank calculates losses at $26.2 billion annually. In this context, Aliko Dangote — Africa's richest man — announced plans to invest in up to 20,000 MW of power generation capacity through the Dangote Group, signalling that the private sector at the highest level recognises both the urgency and the scale of the opportunity. The capital is available. The blueprint has been missing. Until now.
Crisis unresolved — transformation window opens

Human Cost

MEASURED IN LIVES

Economic figures can be recalculated. Lost lives cannot. The power crisis is not merely an infrastructure problem — it is a humanitarian emergency suppressing health outcomes, educational achievement, and basic human dignity across generations.

🏥

HEALTHCARE CRISIS

Over 2,000 health facilities across Nigeria have no reliable electricity. Surgeries performed by torchlight. Vaccines spoiling without refrigeration. Neonatal units running on generators that run dry mid-shift.

Nigeria's maternal mortality rate — 512 per 100,000 live births — is directly linked to healthcare facilities without reliable power (WHO).

ATTRIBUTION
Thousands of preventable deaths annually linked to power-related healthcare failures
📚

EDUCATIONAL STAGNATION

Nigerian children study by candlelight and kerosene lamps. Schools without computers. Universities unable to run research labs. A generation trained to compete digitally, denied access to digital tools by a broken grid.

Only 35% of Nigerian schools have electricity access. Rural areas: under 10%. This educational gap compounds intergenerationally.

SKILL GAP CONSEQUENCE
Major employers — including Dangote Group — cite difficulty recruiting qualified Nigerians. The underpowered education system is a primary cause.
🌾

AGRICULTURAL WASTE

Nigeria wastes 30–40% of its agricultural output annually due to absent cold chain infrastructure. Tomatoes rot in Kano before reaching Lagos. Fish spoils overnight. Grain deteriorates in storage.

Without power, modern irrigation, processing plants, and storage cannot operate. Nigeria imports food it could grow — a direct consequence of energy poverty.

FOOD SECURITY
$9B in annual post-harvest losses attributable to power unavailability in cold chain and processing infrastructure
Generational Impact
Every generation of Nigerians born since independence has grown up in darkness. The child who could not study at night did not reach their potential. The parent who could not cool food lost money they could not afford. The entrepreneur who could not power a machine did not build the business that would have employed others. The power crisis is not a problem to be fixed — it is a wound that has been reopened, every night, for over six decades. AMARA does not propose to patch this wound. It proposes to heal it.

Governance & Corruption

THE CORRUPTION ARCHITECTURE

Nigeria's power sector is not merely technically broken — it is institutionally corrupted. The EFCC Chairman has publicly acknowledged that the level of fraud in the power sector, if fully known to Nigerians, would cause widespread shock. Below is what can be documented.

EFCC Acknowledgement — On Record
The Economic and Financial Crimes Commission has publicly acknowledged that financial fraud, contract inflation, and resource diversion in Nigeria's power sector represents one of the most sophisticated corruption ecosystems in the nation's history. Funds allocated for grid rehabilitation routinely disappear through inflated contracts, ghost vendors, and kickback structures penetrating multiple levels of government and the private sector.
💰
Contract Inflation & Ghost Projects
Between 1999 and 2019, Nigeria allocated over $16 billion to the power sector through various federal programmes. Senate Committee on Power investigations have documented significant portions allocated to projects never commenced, completed sub-standard, or contracted at 3–5× market rates. Independent auditors flagged transformer purchases at prices 400% above global market benchmarks.
$16B+ allocated to the sector; outcomes persistently minimal
📊
Estimated Billing Fraud & Metering Manipulation
With over 45% of consumers unmetered, DisCos use "estimated billing" — consumption approximated without data. In practice, this became systematic over-billing with no consumer recourse. Metered customers report unexplained unit deductions. Band A customers pay premium tariffs for Band D-equivalent service with no regulatory penalty to the DisCo. The absence of smart metering makes fraud nearly undetectable and unliable.
45%+ unmetered = systematic billing fraud enabled at scale
⚡
Bypass Connections & Aggregate Technical Losses
Both supply and demand-side actors participate in the corruption. Businesses and residential communities connect directly to distribution transformers, bypassing meters entirely. DisCos pass these ATC&C losses (Aggregate Technical, Commercial, and Collection) to regulators as infrastructure problems rather than corruption metrics. 30–40% losses represent billions in annually stolen or unaccounted electricity per year.
30–40% ATC&C losses — significantly corruption-driven
🏛️
Privatisation Without Accountability — Profit Privatised, Loss Socialised
The 2013 privatisation generated $2.5 billion. Performance bonds and minimum investment commitments were built into DisCo agreements. Most DisCos have defaulted without consequence. NERC enforcement mechanisms have proven insufficient. The government has repeatedly bailed out DisCos through taxpayer-funded gas supply arrangements and debt forgiveness. A textbook case of privatising profit while socialising loss.
Multiple DisCos in technical default — zero regulatory penalty

No Real-Time Consumption Data
Existing meters are dumb terminals — they count units but provide zero granular data on appliance-level consumption, rate, or time of use.
⚠️
Zero Smart Grid Integration
Current meters cannot communicate with a smart grid system, cannot detect bypass, cannot integrate with demand-response protocols. Fundamentally incompatible with AMARA.

GEOPOLITICAL FACTOR: IRAN / ISRAEL / USA CONFLICT IMPACT

The ongoing Iran-Israel-USA conflict has created significant global energy market disruptions that materially affect the cost, availability, and strategic calculus of Nigeria's power infrastructure investment. This is not peripheral context — it is a core pricing and supply chain risk every serious investor must understand and plan for.

🛢️
Oil & Gas Price Volatility
Brent crude volatility between $75–$95/barrel sustained through 2024–2025. Nigeria's thermal generation costs fluctuate directly with LNG prices, which spiked 15–20% since escalation began. Gas-to-power plant economics worsened.
↑ +15–20% LNG cost impact on thermal generation
🚢
Red Sea Shipping Disruption
Houthi attacks (conflict-linked) on Red Sea shipping rerouted global freight around the Cape of Good Hope — adding 10–14 days and 15%+ in shipping costs for transformers, solar panels, and cables critical to grid development.
↑ +15% equipment import costs; +14 day delivery delays
💵
USD Strength & Naira Pressure
US Fed policy in conflict-driven inflation environment has maintained elevated USD rates. The Naira, already under subsidy-removal pressure, faces additional depreciation risk — raising local-currency cost of all dollar-denominated infrastructure imports by 20–30%.
↑ Import costs elevated 20–30% vs. pre-conflict
☀️
Solar Supply Chain Disruption
Chinese solar manufacturers — dominant globally — face US tariff pressures and geopolitical trade realignments. Lead times for utility-scale solar modules extended from 4 months to 6–9 months. Spot prices up 8–12% from pre-conflict baselines.
↑ Solar module prices +8–12%; procurement timelines extended
🔋
Battery Storage Cost Pressure
Lithium, cobalt, and nickel — critical for utility-scale storage — face export restriction risks from multiple conflict-adjacent supply sources. Battery storage costs remain elevated, with conflict-related supply uncertainty adding a 10–15% risk premium to procurement contracts.
↑ Storage system cost premium: +10–15%
🌱
Green Finance Opportunity
The conflict has accelerated global ESG investment priorities as nations seek energy independence. Nigeria's clean energy commitment, framed correctly, positions AMARA to access IFC, AfDB, and global green bond markets at preferential rates — turning geopolitical disruption into strategic advantage.
